## 01 · COL-314201 · 【3.5】拉莫纳玩法01

*[image: The "Mask" Wearer]*
Sooner or later, a child's rebellious phase comes around, a little storm splitting a once harmonious family with lightning and thunder.
And almost every storm starts the same way, with a determination to go against the mold.
With another exorcism complete, she slips away from the bustling crowd, tucks herself away in a quiet corner, and dozes off.
*[image: Abandoned Building]*
While the exorcist avoids the people, she can't escape the growing hubbub as dawn comes. Cameras flash, journalists scribble, and interviewers stand in attendance.
**Ramona**: Huh, that's weird. The exorcism's over. Why are there still people gathering outside?
She adopted the habits of a night-shift worker at an early age, which by now has made her a full-fledged night owl. Being woken up like this is hardly unusual for her.
**Ramona**: *yawn* I need some more shut-eye.
**Ramona**: Hmm?
Two sets of footsteps echo down the dim hallway. At this hour, anyone coming here is either a ghost, or someone curious about the exorcist herself.
**Judgmental Young Man**: So this is where our "spectacular" exorcist neighbor, Ramona, did the ritual?
**Judgmental Young Man**: Figures she's a fraud. There's nothing here at all! And to think those journalists outside are calling it a miracle from some "family of exorcists"!
**Judgmental Young Man**: Didn't you say earlier Ramona was the real deal? You still sure about that?
**Timid Young Man**: But it's in the newspapers. So, there must be a reason.
**Judgmental Young Man**: That's some dry humor you've got there. My papá says these days, the only people who make the news are the dead and those guys in masks.
**Timid Young Man**: Masks ...
**Judgmental Young Man**: Metaphorically and literally. I heard during exorcisms they paint patterns on their faces to make them look like masks. Just some cheap theatrics.
The boys' voices grow closer. From the shadows, the exorcist hears every word clearly.
She parts her lips and quietly answers their guesses, or rather, their misunderstandings.
**Ramona**: False.
**Timid Young Man**: But, if it's all a scam, why would the city go to all this trouble to hire her? I heard they paid her a lot.
**Judgmental Young Man**: Truth is, it's all a publicity stunt orchestrated by City Hall. Gotta do something to get that investment money.
**Judgmental Young Man**: Hah, times are changing!
**Ramona**: Paid? First I've heard of that.
Amid the boys' chatter, a mix of irritation and betrayal pulls her thoughts back to two days earlier.
*[image: City Government Office]*
**City Official**: Señorita Ramona, are you saying you refuse to carry out this exorcism?
**Ramona**: You heard me. No need to ask twice.
**City Official**: This ritual is being held in the city's name, with even the mayor in attendance. Just think, Señorita Ramona, if this goes smoothly, you'll be a household name overnight!
Ramona's expression remains unchanged. Clearly fame and prestige are no temptation to her.
Used to bargaining in matters like these, the official quickly assumes a reason for her refusal.
**City Official**: Ah, I remember when I was your age, señorita. Back then, I too preferred to do things my own way.
**City Official**: If glory doesn't interest you, then picture this: after the ritual, the merchants will donate a substantial sum, and we'll finally be able to repair this building.
**City Official**: Whatever it becomes—a shopping center or a textile factory—it is sure to be a boost to the economy. What do you say?
**Ramona**: I'm just an exorcist. I don't know anything about the economy.
**City Official**: You're young. It's understandable that you don't see what businesses have done for Mexico. Just look around—railroads, public works, the power grid ... Without funding, would we have ever gotten to this point?
**City Official**: To be frank, we don't care who the money comes from as long as it helps us bring Mexico into the modern age!
**Ramona**: I get it. For this city to modernize, the ghosts've gotta be cleared out.
**City Official**: Exactly!
**Ramona**: Only problem is ...
Sensing another refusal coming, the official quickly pulls out a document.
**City Official**: People died there. Troublemakers. We need to make a statement. This is the exorcism contract, already signed by your parents. It's perfectly valid.
**Ramona**: They signed it, not me. You've got the wrong person.
**City Official**: I figured you'd say as much. But your parents left the country overnight for reasons we can't disclose.
**City Official**: It's already been announced that you'll perform the ritual. If you back out now, your family's reputation is finished.
Ramona's eyes grow even more weary, her defiance noticeably easing. Like accepting a school disciplinary notice, she takes the agreement.
**Ramona**: Those two cowards ...
**City Official**: What was that?
**Ramona**: Nothing. I'll be there.
**Timid Young Man**: Still, I heard ordinary people hire her too. To calm the spirits of dead pets and things like that.
**Judgmental Young Man**: That's bizarre! Our pets are Mexican too, you know. Their souls make their own way to the Sun, Mictlan, or Tlalocan, just like ours do when we die. But those frauds claim they're evil spirits and destroy them before they can even get there.
**Timid Young Man**: Your papá told you that?
**Judgmental Young Man**: Of course. He knows everything, except how rotten those phony exorcists are.
The subject of their rumors stays hidden in a corner. She shrugs them off, partly from drowsiness, partly from being used to it.
**Ramona**: I've heard it all before. People don't know the first thing about spirits.
**Judgmental Young Man**: Are you shaking?
**Timid Young Man**: Um, actually, I've been hearing someone talking this whole time.
**Ramona**: Ramona suddenly perks up.
**Judgmental Young Man**: Fraidy-cat. You're just imagining it.
**Judgmental Young Man**: Besides, there aren't any evil ghosts here!
**Timid Young Man**: I-I'm not sure.
**Ramona**: **He's right. It's not his imagination. I've been watching you.**
The voice from the darkness freezes them instantly. They turn to each other and scream, their panicked footsteps quickly echoing into obscurity.
**Ramona**: It was just a joke. What's all the panic for?
New footsteps sound behind her. With a sliver of curiosity, Ramona turns.
Knowing the building as well as she does, she's certain the newcomer is a living being.
A figure slowly emerges from the dark, revealing shiny dress shoes, a flared skirt, and a girl's faintly provocative stare.
**???**: So you really are here.
**Ramona**: You know me?
Her flat tone carries no sarcasm, yet somehow it irritates the other girl even more than if it did.
**???**: Don't play dumb. I go to St. Ignatius too. Renée, remember? We're classmates.
**Ramona**: I remember. You spoke to me last week.
Renée raises a brow, satisfied.
**Ramona**: "Stay away from me, you creep. Don't ever talk to me again." Pretty sure those were the words.
Usually the vengeful type, Renée only frowns.
**Renée**: Those boys just now, they your new "friends"? That's a relief. At least you're not playing with ghosts anymore.
**Ramona**: What do you want, Renée?
This, in truth, is why Renée dislikes Ramona. Her solitary, indifferent nature makes the social struggles of her peers feel childish.
**Renée**: I want your help. Whatever the mayor's paying you, I'll match it.
**Ramona**: I'm not getting paid for this job.
**Renée**: What? Why?
**Ramona**: Uh, "for the modernization of Mexico," or some big ideas like that.
**Renée**: How can you work for free? I ought to tell Mamá; she'll be sure to have plenty to say about that!
Her misplaced concern makes the moment awkward.
**Renée**: Ahem, don't get the wrong idea. I'm just standing up for fairness.
**Ramona**: Got it.
**Ramona**: Look, I don't get why you care all of a sudden, but you should be heading home for breakfast. Lights are up, which means I gotta sleep.
**Renée**: W-Wait!
But years of exorcist training have made Ramona light on her feet. She vaults the railing and vanishes without a sound, the truth behind the contract vanishing with her.
There were no ghosts in this building. The "exorcism" was nothing more than ...
... a pretentious trick.

## 02 · COL-314202 · 【3.5】拉莫纳玩法02

*[image: The Temptation of Happiness]*
*[image: Ramona's Home]*
On this unusually quiet morning, Ramona sets aside her agave incense and briefly returns to ordinary life. But the letters on the table nag at her like a flock of noisy chachalacas.
Making breakfast, washing dishes, sorting clothes ... She puts off opening the envelopes with any task she can. But in the end, the bright stamp stuck to the one on top wins.
As usual, inside is not only a letter, but a photograph.
She flips it over, revealing familiar handwriting and little doodles, tinged with the ambition and optimism she never inherited.
**Ramona**: "We've arrived in Jamaica. Unlucky for us, this ghost cat proved too tricky. If only you were here, you'd have been able to lift the curse."
**Ramona**: A ghost cat? What's that?
**Ramona**: Forget it. They probably just heard it somewhere. They can't even see ghosts.
—Which is exactly why they left right after signing the contract.
Her parents took the commission that should have been hers and trotted off to somewhere else in the world. The "exorcist family" scam continues, and the consequences fall on her once again.
After a moment, she opens another letter. The tidy handwriting and earnest advice seem to find a voice, murmuring in her ear.
**Man's Voice**: "It's time you started behaving better at St. Ignatius. Stop telling your teachers there are ghosts sitting on their shoulders, and don't tell your classmates the truth about what's in the restroom."
**Ramona**: But half the time I'm not even trying to scare them.
**Woman's Voice**: "Learn to refuse requests to perform exorcisms at night. It isn't good for your health."
**Ramona**: Most people say they're busy during the day. Even if they're scared outta their minds, they still say I gotta come after dark.
**Ramona**: Besides, wasn't it you who said it was money over anything?
**Man's Voice**: "You're older now and, of course, have every right to make your own decisions. But do you really want to live this lonely life forever?"
**Ramona**: It is lonely, but I don't mind it.
**Man's Voice**: "Others look down on you because you don't treat exorcisms as serious work. You have no friends, no home, none of the comforts of life. This is no way to live!"
**Woman's Voice**: "Worst of all, mi amor, you will always be thought of as a fraud. But your father and I know how honest you are. You'd rather stay silent before a spirit than say something hurtful."
**Ramona**: It's not honesty. I just can't talk to them. I said it a thousand times.
**Ramona**: And you don't have to hear a ghost to exorcise 'em anyway. It's about skills, not chatter.
**Woman's Voice**: "Talent is destiny, mi amor. The 'spirit sight' that never awakened in us came to you like a miracle. You must treasure it and accomplish great things."
**Ramona**: Destiny ... We all die eventually. If anything is predetermined, it's death.
**Woman's Voice**: "I know, I know, 'everyone dies eventually.' But you're only fifteen. You mustn't be so pessimistic!"
Even through the letter, they predict her answer. Blood ties.
**Ramona**: ...
Irritated, she crumples the letter—an action she's done so many times it's become second nature.
**Ramona**: You seriously telling me I gotta lie to live a good life?
**Ramona**: Besides, what even counts as a "good" life?
Her frustration finds no answer. And worse, she has no one to talk it out with.
She looks at the sky outside and decides to go out and find somewhere quiet.
Mexico's cities are changing quickly these days. Many people have moved into new homes, leaving some ghosts without one.
*[image: Morelos District]*
For nearly a week, someone has been following her. She's known for a while who it is from the footsteps alone.
And if she doesn't deal with them now, she'll never have any peace.
**Ramona**: I know you're there. Come out.
As usual, the haughty confidence of her stalker causes her to appear around the corner shiny-shoes-first.
**Renée**: You're pretty observant.
**Ramona**: What do you want?
**Renée**: I already told you. I want your help.
**Ramona**: Yeah, and I said no.
**Renée**: That's none of my concern.
The blunt reply leaves Ramona momentarily speechless. After all, conversation isn't exactly her strong suit.
**Renée**: Just drive the evil spirit out of my house, and I'll pay you a tidy sum. Name your price.
**Ramona**: ...
**Renée**: I may have only been at school for two months, but I already know you're capable.
**Renée**: Or do you care more about reputation? I can go all around the city singing your praises if you like.
Renée smooths her skirt and clears her throat, as if preparing to step onto a stage.
**Renée**: Come on, please, Ramona! Those evil spirits are causing such a ruckus that my papá is even planning to move us out of the city!
**Renée**: I simply can't leave, I can't! I've just made friends! If I leave them now, nothing but a barren, empty future awaits me!
**Ramona**: ...
**Ramona**: Isn't that a bit dramatic?
**Renée**: Maybe for everyday life, but for a performance it's rather good, I'd say. I think I've got it to be rather convincing, don't you?
**Ramona**: That's not what I meant.
**Renée**: Regardless, everything I said was true, especially the part about my friends. You probably wouldn't understand, ever "brave" and "independent" as you are.
Memories of her upbringing catch up to her, and she feels a little embarrassed at her own sharpness. She steadies herself. This is hardly how you ask someone for help.
**Renée**: Anyway, I really did come to you sincerely.
**Ramona**: No, I don't doubt that. It's just ... right now, people in this city are all wound up.
**Ramona**: It's probably just a wandering spirit that lost its way and ended up in your home. They usually leave after a few days.
**Ramona**: I don't think we really need to destroy them all.
Her honesty earns no trust. After all, it has never been a particularly persuasive virtue.
**Renée**: I hear your parents have been pulling all kinds of strings to try and make you into a famous exorcist. I can help too. You'd make a fortune.
Renée notices Ramona's eyes flicker and assumes she's reconsidering.
**Ramona**: This isn't going anywhere. Please, step aside, Renée.
**Renée**: I don't get it. Who would refuse terms like that? Do you hate me so much?
**Ramona**: Nah. It's just exorcisms shouldn't come tied to worldly rewards.
Ramona turns to leave again. She's always been good at avoiding problems, just like when she left home after getting that letter.
**Renée**: Wait!
**Renée**: Things at home are just awful right now. If you fix it, I'll even be your friend. I know somewhere under there you envy people like us—those of us who have someone to spend their days with.
A spoiled teen and a reclusive loner, both unaware of the sting in their words. For adolescents, this is hardly a bad thing.
Ramona turns away, letting her back give the answer.
**Renée**: You've not gotten rid of me! I'll be back!
In the distance, Ramona stops and turns around.
Later, Renée will often recall this moment when the understated exorcist showed a surprising softness.
Of course, Ramona herself doesn't think much at all about it.
**Ramona**: What was that?
**Ramona**: Fine. I'll deal with the spirit if you stop bothering me.
**Renée**: You mean ...
**Ramona**: Lead the way.
**Ramona**: And quit crying, yeah? People will think I scared a classmate again.
**Renée**: Right. This way!

## 03 · COL-314203 · 【3.5】拉莫纳玩法03

*[image: Parrot]*
*[image: Uptown, Mexico City]*
**Journalist**: I'm not doubting your judgment, but we've already been burned too many times by exorcists.
**Journalist**: It would be safer to hire one we know, or have your father's associates recommend someone.
**Renée**: Mamá, that's precisely what you said the last time. And do you recall how that turned out?
**Journalist**: Mi amor, those were just accidents. At least we've had three peaceful nights, haven't we?
The exorcist at hand stands by the door, quietly listening to their argument. Her calm refusal to argue and lack of any kind of sales pitch finally draw the mother's attention.
**Journalist**: Alright, Renée, you've convinced me. Now, if you'd please introduce me to your, uh, classmate.
**Renée**: Her name's Ramona. She handled that exorcism a few days ago—the one that both solved an urgent problem for the mayor and secured reconstruction funding.
**Renée**: Mamá, weren't you planning to interview her?
**Journalist**: What?
The look of excited surprise on her face is nothing new to Ramona. She knows exactly what thoughts are hiding behind it—reputation, prestige, the very things hinted at in her parents' letter.
**Journalist**: But you're just a kid. I'd expected someone older.
**Ramona**: Yeah, you and the rest. People usually think I'm some kind of legendary old shaman or something.
**Journalist**: Sorry, this spirit has caused quite the ruckus. I had to delay our interview.
**Journalist**: But, since you're here, perhaps we could conduct it during the exorcism? I've already prepared an outline.
A good journalist never wastes an opportunity.
**Ramona**: Sorry, señora, but I have to say no.
**Ramona**: I'm here to perform an exorcism. Nothing else.
**Renée**: Forget it, Mamá. She's as stubborn as a desert cactus. You'll only get pricked.
**Journalist**: Alright. Any journalist worth their salt knows to respect boundaries. I shan't ruin the atmosphere.
**Ramona**: Can I come in now?
**Journalist**: Of course.
*[image: Living Room]*
The living room is bright and tidy, but books and framed paintings lie scattered across the floor, as if a scuffle happened and the owner never had time to clean up.
In the corners the light can't reach, Ramona senses something restless, something not of this world.
**Ramona**: If I'd known your house had this many guests, I wouldn't have come.
**Renée**: Hm? But there's nobody here.
In a serious setting, dry humor always lands a moment late.
**Renée**: Aah! Where are they?
**Ramona**: Relax.
Both mother and daughter catch the note of satisfaction in the exorcist's voice at their reaction. Strangely, they don't dislike it.
In fact, it makes her seem less distant.
**Ramona**: Tell me what's been happening these past few days.
**Renée**: I thought you'd know just by looking.
**Ramona**: It's not that simple. Different people feel spirits differently. I need to understand how they're affecting you.
**Journalist**: Let me think. At first, book pages folded themselves into paper airplanes and flew around the house.
**Renée**: Right! At first, Mamá thought it was a surprise I'd prepared for her, until she realized they just kept flying around and around!
**Ramona**: A surprise?
**Journalist**: Yes. Renée has some arcanist blood. Her father and I always hoped her power would awaken some day.
**Journalist**: So, I suppose in blind hope, we were quick to misread strange things. When she scraped her knee as a child, we even encouraged her to heal it with her mind alone.
**Journalist**: Naive, isn't it? Like something out of a cartoon.
No one notices Ramona drift off for a moment.
**Ramona**: Tell me what else has been going on.
**Journalist**: The phonograph turns on by itself and plays music that was never recorded.
**Journalist**: Honestly, the songs are beautiful. If only they didn't play in the middle of the night.
**Ramona**: All pretty typical so far.
**Renée**: There's more! Marigolds sprout out of the floor every night. It's like it's the Day of the Dead every day in our house.
**Renée**: And glass jars keep shattering for no reason. We've got nowhere to store candy, flour, or cookies anymore.
**Ramona**: Sounds like the spirits really don't like you.
**Renée**: We didn't invite you here to insult us.
**Ramona**: You mentioned "three peaceful nights." Could you elaborate?
**Renée**: These things kept happening for a month. The only quiet nights were when an exorcist stayed awake watching over us.
**Renée**: Of course, she eventually left to go rest, and then everything went back to this again.
**Ramona**: A month? That is a pretty long time.
**Ramona**: Small pranks, repeated behavior, and no serious physical or mental harm. Got it.
She raises the agave incense in her hand, the ember glowing brighter and redder.
To the two clients, the laid-back air that set her apart from the city has vanished, replaced by a sharp decisiveness rare for someone her age.
**Renée**: Have you figured something out?
**Ramona**: Maybe. What did the last exorcists say?
**Renée**: Hmph, most of them said the only solution was some expensive warding tools, but none of them worked.
**Renée**: The worst was when my papá bought half a warehouse of corn kernels from a fortune-teller.
**Renée**: Those kernels didn't tell us anything at all! It hasn't gotten us any closer to this ghost's identity or purpose!
**Ramona**: Can't say it isn't creative.
**Renée**: I told you exorcists were all frauds!
**Journalist**: Oh, I'm sure Renée didn't mean to offend you, Señorita Ramona.
**Ramona**: Me? I didn't take it that way.
**Journalist**: Good. As you know, exorcists are quite popular these days, and many of your peers are sensitive to criticism.
**Journalist**: Public opinion matters. Especially for someone from an "exorcist family."
The journalist pauses meaningfully, her eyes locked on Ramona.
**Ramona**: You trying to test how I feel about this stuff, señora?
**Journalist**: My, you are perceptive. Sorry, habit of the profession.
**Ramona**: I'm not a parrot. I'm not gonna say what you wanna hear. Sorry.
The simple metaphor seems to have done its job, leaving the journalist momentarily silent.
Interpreting the awkward silence as a setback, Renée starts to grow anxious.
**Renée**: Mamá, you're getting off topic!
**Journalist**: You're right. Let's get back to the exorcism.
**Ramona**: To get to the point, these spirits don't care one bit about public opinion.
**Ramona**: And if public opinion could sway spirits, we wouldn't have so many problems.
**Journalist**: Hah, you're funny.
**Ramona**: Thanks. Not a lot of people get that about me.
**Journalist**: ...
**Ramona**: Time to get started. If you're scared, you can wait outside.
**Journalist**: Let's step out, Renée, for respect's sake.
Her gaze fixes on a corner of the ceiling, where several spirits drift.
The disturbances around them are violent. Not gentle ripples, but the thrashing of someone drowning.
**Ramona**: Not a usual state for a spirit to be in ...
She blows lightly on the agave incense. The smoke spreads, revealing the trails of the spirits to her eyes only.
**Ramona**: They came from outside.
**Ramona**: Then, are they attached to this house, or seeking revenge on the residents?
She lights a candle with the ember and releases her arcane energy through it, testing their reaction.
The spirits only grow more agitated, even trying to snuff out the flame.
**Ramona**: Refusing to talk, then purification it is.
**Ramona**: I'll send you away and end the suffering of being trapped here.

**Renée**: She opens the door, the bright sunlight dizzying her for a moment. The clients stand outside, anxious looks on their faces.

And the clients weren't just being polite, either.

**Journalist**: Gosh, doesn't the air feel fresher?
**Ramona**: Not really. Probably just the lingering incense.
**Renée**: Want some nut brittle?
**Ramona**: Sure. Thanks.

**Ramona**: That's 'cause most exorcists claim they can do that, so people go assuming I'm the same.
**Ramona**: While we're at it, let me clarify that spirits aren't good or evil, either. They've just lost their way in the mortal world. Once their energy runs out, they fade away on their own.
**Journalist**: I see. Then allow me to give a proper response.
**Journalist**: We bought this house two months ago. For years, Renée's father's business struggled, and we moved constantly.
**Renée**: Mamá, you didn't have to bring that up!
**Journalist**: My dear, we must be honest about our past. You can't simply avoid it because we have a better life now.
**Renée**: That's not what I meant.
**Journalist**: Señorita Ramona, I could tell the moment you arrived that Renée practically dragged you here, didn't she?
**Ramona**: Pretty much.
**Journalist**: Please forgive her. She just doesn't want to move again.
**Journalist**: Every move means more lost friends and another difficult beginning. I imagine you understand that.
Ramona says nothing.
To Renée, these words sound like a provocation. After all, it wasn't so long ago that she said the very same thing to try and hurt Ramona.
**Renée**: Mamá, why would you say that?
**Journalist**: Hm? Did I say something wrong?
**Ramona**: No.
Sensing the awkwardness, the journalist deftly changes the subject.
**Journalist**: By the way, about that card. It came from a partner of mine. The publishing house is running a call for essays about Mexico over the past decade.
**Journalist**: As a Mexicana myself, I have some thoughts I'd like to put to paper.
**Journalist**: I care deeply about this land, and I want my words to enter public discourse, but my own observations aren't enough. I need to learn more.
**Journalist**: From people like you, for example. Your silence could be a powerful entry point for an argument.
**Ramona**: No. I'm just not really a fan of small talk.
**Renée**: Mamá, stop talking about work!
For the first time, Ramona begins to understand the source of Renée's often-irritated nature.
And at the same time, Renée begins to understand why Ramona keeps to herself.
**Journalist**: Alright, alright, no need to get upset, Renée.
**Journalist**: I've got some ideas I need to jot down. I'll be in my room.
**Renée**: You should know, I didn't invite you just to let Mamá interrogate you.
**Renée**: You're just so famous these days, and she happens to be in a slump.
**Ramona**: I know. You wouldn't think that far ahead.
**Renée**: ...
**Renée**: You can be pretty cutting when you want to be, you know.
The herbal scent in the air fades, and the mood cools with it.
**Renée**: I heard you live alone while your parents travel the world. Not everyone has that kind of freedom.
**Ramona**: Freedom ...
**Ramona**: Actually, my parents are like yours, always trying to get people in on their "profound truths."
**Renée**: They write to you a lot, don't they?
**Ramona**: How'd you know that?
**Ramona**: Oh, right. You were following me.
**Renée**: I was not!
**Renée**: Hmph, you completely ignored me at school. So I had no choice but to come to your house!
**Ramona**: Did I really?
**Renée**: ...
**Renée**: What do they write about?
**Renée**: I'm not prying, just trying to move on to a new topic!
**Ramona**: They tell me to behave better at school and to cozy up with important people, that's about it.
**Renée**: Well, you know my mamá now, and she's a famous journalist. That ought to satisfy them.
**Ramona**: Yeah, as long as I don't have to be the one to tell 'em.
**Renée**: So they make you pursue things you're not interested in too?
**Ramona**: Make me? I guess. But they've eased up lately.
**Renée**: How did you manage that?
**Ramona**: It only took a few attempts at running away from home. Eventually they gave up.
**Renée**: That's actually a good idea.
**Ramona**: Renée, I should go.
**Ramona**: No need to pretend we're best friends, yeah?
Renée stands and glances toward her mother's study. Her sleep-deprived mind is swirling with historical events, sparing no attention for the small friendship forming in the living room.
**Renée**: We're settling per job, right?
**Ramona**: Mhm.
Renée hands the envelope of payment to her.
They're like two children playing adults. The absurdity makes Renée laugh.
**Renée**: I'll see you out. It's stuffy in here anyway. I want some air.
**Ramona**: Alright.
**Ramona**: But ...
She studies the girl for a moment, choosing her words carefully.
**Ramona**: Renée, you seem different from how you were back in school.
**Renée**: I'll take that as a compliment.

**Black Ibis**: Letters from a friend? Had a falling out?
**Ramona**: Friend? They're from my parents.
**Black Ibis**: Your parents? I see. Then, I'll treat this as another one of your secrets.
**Ramona**: Appreciate it, boss.
**Ramona**: If it's not too much trouble for you, I'll just use the Bureau's address from now on. The mailman can deliver them there. I won't read them anyway.
**Black Ibis**: Sure. Once you officially join, you'll have a desk and a mailbox.
**Ramona**: That's the real reason you came here today, isn't it?
**Black Ibis**: You got me. Wouldn't want to miss out on new talent.
**Ramona**: I don't like the idea of being a Vigil.
**Ramona**: A contractor's life is just right for me. Low commitment. Besides, I've never been good at dealing with beings that talk.
**Black Ibis**: Fair enough. The Bureau can develop a partnership model as well.
**Ramona**: Thanks for understanding, boss. I gotta go. There's a job waiting.
**Black Ibis**: Adiós, Ramona.
**Ramona**: Adiós, boss.
