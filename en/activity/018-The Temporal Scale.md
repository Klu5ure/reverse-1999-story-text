## 02 · Trail · Speech Duel

*[image: Q&A with the Checkered Gray-and-White]*

First Meeting Minutes on the Point Irreversible

Committee Member A: I maintain that exploring the Point Irreversible now is reckless. It would waste time and resources we don't have to spare. These are extraordinary times, Ms. Kenna.

Kenna: Precisely because these are extraordinary times, Committee Member. We can't afford the luxury of overlooking a known exception.

Committee Member A: I think we have different definitions of waste, Ms. Kenna. I cannot agree to a risk like this—

Kenna: I prefer to call it necessary sowing, Committee Member. Plant a seed, and soon enough, it bears fruit.

Committee Member B: You've spent too long in Glasgow, Ms. Kenna. Does everyone there speak in metaphors?

Kenna: Would you like to discuss rhetoric with me, Committee Member? I could start with Georgian literature—though I doubt either of us wants that.

Kenna: I believe surveying the Point Irreversible is necessary, and it will shape what comes next.

Kenna: One Point Irreversible means there could be more. As you said, our focus is fighting against Manus Vindictae. That's exactly why we need these strongholds, Committee Member.

Kenna: We can't be an island in times like these.

Kenna: Sending a boat out toward the Point Irreversible is the first step to making sure we're not.

## 11 · Trail · Read Something Fresh

*[image: Sequel · UTTU Scouting Trip]*

Crossing most of that vast building—the long corridors, the broad staircases, the open ground beneath its fallen dome—left me nearly spent. You can probably guess how I felt. I checked my notebook of hurried sketches one last time and told myself: "Just a little further—that tavern drink the locals talked about is waiting for you."

Unsurprisingly, the "neighbors" were at the tavern too. The old woman behind the bar mixed me a quick drink. Beside me sat a man in an old postal uniform, blank-faced, his shoulders worn pale. A ragged, half-transparent canvas mailbag rested on his lap. It seemed I had a "ghost" for company.

"You're welcome to move seats if he makes you uneasy. Old Michał never listens to anyone anyway," the old woman offered gently.

I thanked her but stayed put. It was, after all, a good chance to rest while observing a "ghost." The old postman's shade sat in silence, staring at the grain of the bar counter, as if an after-work vodka should have been waiting there. Not far off, an old woman wrapped in a heavy shawl stood by a table, taking out the same photo—a photo-shaped shade—again and again, turning it over in her hands. I guessed she must have lived alone in some apartment block, in a room that always smelled of coal stove and old cloth.

By the time I finished my drink and looked back, a man in work clothes was standing at the center of the empty tavern—I couldn't say when he'd arrived—as if he'd been waiting there for a hundred years, a thousand years. Half his face was burned, and I caught the faint scent of ash coming off him.

Something about his face must have drawn me in, because I found myself standing, trying to speak with him.

"Sir, if I may ask, what are you doing here?"

"Waiting." His answer came out hoarse.

"Waiting for whom?"

"A woman with eyes as blue as Lake Baikal."

"You're hurt, sir."

"No ... this isn't a wound."

"It's Her ... it's proof that God has forsaken us."

He raised a hand to the burn scarring his cheek.

"We were promised gold. That year, winter went on forever. I waved her goodbye and headed into the frozen wild.

The workers labored on, day and night.

Until the black oil erupted from the wells, flames rising and fading in the falling snow."

His pupils trembled faintly, a silent fire burning somewhere in him.

"Our sky broke open—but no dawn ever came."

I think I knew where he'd come from. Everyone in this country knew where he and his scars came from.

"While we were surveying the building, we came across a man with burn scars on his face ... He told us he was waiting for a woman with eyes as blue as Lake Baikal."

"Ah. So you've already met Tomasz."

Another rustle of cloth. Knuckles clenching. A soft sigh.

"He's always wandering this building—the stairs, the landings, my tavern. He's still wearing that old wool coat he had on the day I saw him off at the station.

Even though he's long dead. What wanders here is just his ghost, his echo, whatever you want to call it—repeating the same few lines over and over even when I'm standing right in front of him."

"I'm so ..."

"No need to apologize, Stasia. You found my pendant for me—if anything, I owe you a few more drinks.

"Don't worry ... that's just how life goes. Tomasz proposed to me once. He wanted to go work the far-off oil fields and save up enough to come back and marry me. He never made it back. When they brought me the scraps of his clothes, I thought that was the end of everything. When I was exiled, I thought that was the end too ... And when the anomaly hit, I really thought it was over this time. But here I am. Even Tomasz is still here.

"So ... life still goes on."

## 22 · Trail · Childhood Fragment

*[image: A Page in the Diary]*

Sat Sunny

We got a rare good day today! The sky's still gray, but I could actually see the sun. I woke up early, since Róża and I had agreed to meet at the flowerbed. I'd packed sliced black bread and pickles into my bag the night before. I couldn't wait, and after breakfast I ran straight there. Róża was already waiting for me, but Emil and Andrzej were late. We waited ten minutes for them, and Róża talked with me the whole time.

We took the tram to the newly built steel factory. Emil and Andrzej wanted to see the chimney, and I wanted to sketch. It really was a huge chimney, like some beast breathing coal-fire smoke from its great mouth. Róża said that after all these years, Nowa Miedź finally having a factory this size is a good thing. I drew the chimney, the canal, and a few wildflowers. My river sketchbook keeps getting thicker. Andrzej said he thinks I'll become a river expert. But it's not the canal itself I want to draw, though it matters too, of course. Why do we build our houses by the river? Why do we build our factories by the river? Why does the canal bring more people here? Why does the river turn black? Could we live somewhere better? Róża's explained it all to me before, but I want to find the answers myself. Maybe once I've figured it all out, I can help Mum, Dad, and my friends live better lives.

## 28 · Trail · Long Journey

*[image: The Story of a Brick]*

When I was still a student, I spent a year traveling, visiting buildings. I wandered the abandoned amphitheaters of Rome, its aqueducts and temples, sketching in the Mediterranean wind the way Brunelleschi and Donatello did in the 15th century. I was trying to find the essence, the true form of architecture, in those collapsed, unadorned ruins.

Until at last I arrived in India. At the Indian Institute of Management, I met an old man with scars on his face. He told me a story about a brick and an arch.

The architect asks the brick, "Brick, what do you want to be?"

The brick says, "I want to be an arch."

The architect says, "I want an arch too, but arches are too expensive. What if we use a concrete lintel instead?"

Still, the brick tells you only this: "I want to be an arch."

At the end of the story, the old man said, "It's important to respect the material you've chosen. If you're going to use this brick, the best way is to respect it, to let it become what it is, not just to make do with it."

## 31 · Trail · New Life

*[image: A Hidden Letter]*

Dear Olga,

My warmest greetings to you, old friend!

You might be surprised to get this letter. Marek and I wrote it together because we thought you'd want to know how we're doing. And we wanted to talk to you too—to our dear old neighbor, someone who still remembers that we come from Nowa Miedź.

We're doing well in Gdańsk. The sky isn't gray here. We've rented a house near the north port and started a small business. Please, don't worry about us.

It's hard to put into words how I survived those first years. At least three times a week, I'd wake up crying because I'd dreamed of that day, the last time I saw Lucjan. Even now, I still feel sick when I see one of those cattle cars. It was cars just like that one that swallowed my child. They split families up between different groups, and by the time I finally got off that train alive, I'd lost my son forever.

Forgive me for writing about such painful things. But Lucjan loved you so much. I think about it often—if we could go back to Nowa Miedź, how he'd run straight into your arms, how he'd introduce you to his little sister. He never got to meet her. Maybe he's watching her from somewhere above.

Which reminds me, I should introduce you to the newest member of our family—Maja. She was born two winters ago. I've included a photo of her with this letter. I wanted you to see her for yourself. She has my nose, Marek's eyes, and ears just like Lucjan's, the same shape that came down from my grandmother. I think Lucjan would be happy for us. He always wanted a little sister.

We asked around for a long time, and finally a fellow countryman told us your address hadn't changed at all—still the one I remembered. I can't really describe how that felt to hear. You're a brave woman, Olga, and that's such a rare thing these days. We've all weathered the storm. No one escapes its ravages. I wish you health and good fortune, and that you and your family are well. Marek and I think of you often, and we hope that someday we'll see each other again.

Always thinking of you,

Katarzyna

## 32 · Trail · The Wake

*[image: A Late Funeral]*

They left a small gap around the doors and windows. This building has plenty of windows. If it could hold voices in, then departing souls should be able to pass through those same gaps and reach heaven, or so the elders said. The young architect nodded and covered the mirror, as her hometown's traditions demanded—so the living wouldn't glimpse their reflection and trap the dead among them.

Someone should have done this for that boy years ago. He was left behind in the train car, among the exiled, in the very building he'd once lived in. This was a funeral long overdue.

Jasio put on his best suit, the only foreign wool one he owned. Everyone who'd known the boy dressed in their finest. There was no coffin at this funeral, but people still gathered at the little bar and kept vigil for the boy all through the night. Candlelight rose soft and warm, lighting up the seat where he used to sit.

"You're singing," said the girl with the hand puppet. "What should I sing?"

They taught her how to sing a dirge. A wake needed no instruments, only song after song.

At dawn, the people set out from the bar with white candles in hand, heading for the dome. The boy had wanted to see that dome finished.

"Say something, Stasia. He'd want to hear it from you," the old woman said softly.

The architect stepped forward and cleared her throat. The words caught there like fine sand—an itch she couldn't clear.

"I won't forget Lucjan." She began with those words, as sunlight spilled gently through the broken dome, and it made her think of the light that boy had become. "He ... he's with us."

## 41 · Trail · Before the Rain

*[image: "Living Electricity System"]*

"If blood turns to electric current, does the soul still stay in the body?"

The blood vessels change first. They stop their soft pulsing and begin to grow straight, silent, climbing upward along the wrists—red, yellow, blue, green, black, and white gelcoat sheathing thin threads of metal, pushing slowly through skin, through flesh.

The bakery clerk is the first to notice something wrong. She stops touching hot water. When the morning steam rises, her arms give off a faint glow, and in the places where blood should flow, something else now coils and branches quietly, like a tangle of wiring rewoven into place.

More and more people fill the hospitals. X-rays line the light boxes. Inside the pale bodies there are no organs, only crisscrossing wires, like the vast, silent power grid beneath a city. The doctors say the humming in the chest, the numbness in the neck, and the teeth that flicker with light now and then—all of it is nothing but insomnia.

Then even the chapel starts fizzing and crackling with prayer. The silver ornament on the host's chest is leaking current, throwing off tiny blue sparks. Someone weeps on their knees. Someone catches the smell of scorched metal in the air. An old woman looks up and quietly asks the question that has long lingered within her heart:

"If blood turns to electric current, does the soul still stay in the body?"

The host doesn't answer. A patch of bronze has surfaced on his palm too.

## 45 · Trail · Official Document

*[image: Record on the Requisition of the Nowa Miedź Point Irreversible Building and the Establishment of the Eastern Europe Branch]*

Clearance Level: Top Secret

Record on the Requisition of the Nowa Miedź Point Irreversible Building and the Establishment of the Eastern Europe Branch

Attendee List: Lucy, Irinei, Kenna, Ida, Finn ... (and other authorized associated personnel). All personnel listed above must strictly comply with the confidentiality agreement.

In accordance with the Temporal Anomaly Regional Intervention Regulations and related requisition management procedures, the following is recorded regarding the elevation of the former "St. Pavlov Foundation Nowa Miedź Office" to the status of Eastern Europe Branch:

I. Background

The Temporal Anomaly is a highly dangerous arcane spatiotemporal hazard. Any individual exposed within its area of effect will vanish instantly, without a trace.

A building in the Nowa Miedź region holds extremely high strategic value. It has been confirmed that this building is capable of stably resisting the regressive effects of the Temporal Anomaly. Individuals within its enclosed structure are immune to the Temporal Anomaly's effects.

II. Establishment of the Office

On November 8, 1996, the St. Pavlov Foundation Nowa Miedź Office was formally completed. Its primary responsibilities include monitoring abnormal environmental energy, coordinating resident evacuations, and handling regional anomalous incidents.

Across multiple disaster events, this facility has operated stably, with no reversion breach incidents recorded.

III. Formal Requisition

On January 18, 1986, the Foundation formally initiated the permanent requisition process, bringing the building under core facility management. Structural and functional renovations followed, including a rebuilt disaster-prevention system, the addition of research and containment areas, and safety upgrades to public spaces.

All former residents were properly resettled, and the corresponding community functions were transferred accordingly.

IV. Establishment of the Branch

On ██ ██, 19██, all renovation work was completed. With a portion of the public space preserved, the site was formally established as:

"St. Pavlov Foundation Eastern Europe Branch"

The former designation, "Nowa Miedź Office," is discontinued as of this date. All related files and authorizations are consolidated into the Eastern Europe Branch system.

Recorded accordingly.

St. Pavlov Foundation Central Council

██ ██, 19██

## 46 · Trail · Growth of Order

*[image: Excerpt from the Storm Act (Temporary)]*

Excerpt from the Storm Act (Temporary)

Before the term "Storm" was formally established, the Foundation used "Temporal Anomaly" as the umbrella term for this class of phenomena. Anomalous incidents, including time reversal, the disappearance of buildings, the erasure of populations, and historical discontinuities, were filed separately for a long time. It was only after the Nowa Miedź Project that the Foundation confirmed these were manifestations of the same systemic disaster and began drafting the first edition of the "Storm" Regional Intervention Regulations.

I. Confirmation of Terminology

The "Storm" is provisionally confirmed as a highly dangerous disaster whose core manifestations are time reversal, historical erosion, and the reconstruction of reality.

"Temporal Anomaly" is retained solely as a legacy term from earlier records.

...

II. Duties of the Foundation

The Supplementary Act on the Foundation's Duties specifies the following:

The Foundation bears responsibility for the research and early warning of the "Storm," the takeover of endangered zones, personnel evacuation, the preservation of historical civilizations, and the humanitarian restoration of survivors' identities.

The primary bearer of responsibility and the details of implementation remain under dispute. The portions that have been finalized are recorded as follows:

...

At this stage, "Storm" research prioritizes early-warning mechanisms, with continued work toward advancing and refining the "Storm" emergency response system, and ongoing exploration into the possibility of crossing or ending the "Storm" ...

...

A system of disclosure rights concerning "Storm"-related information should be established, with strict controls against leaks of classified information, and close attention paid to staff panic, despair, and other emotional strain ...

...
